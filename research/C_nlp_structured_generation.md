## 2. Findings (one paragraph each)

**F1. Outcome-verified self-training: STaR, RFT, ReST-EM, expert iteration. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Mechanism: sample several outputs per training input, keep those a verifier accepts (here: solver optimum equals the label), fine-tune, repeat. STaR (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465) [S] adds "rationalization" (re-sampling with the answer as a hint) and reports comparable accuracy to a 30x larger model on CommonsenseQA; RFT (https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01825) [S] finds gains grow with the number of *distinct* correct paths, smaller models gain more, and pooling rejection samples from several models lifts LLaMA-7B GSM8K from 35.9 (SFT) to 49.3; ReST-EM (https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06585) [S] is generate-filter-fine-tune repeated "a few times", scales with model size, and its authors flag false positives (right feedback, wrong solution). That each round restarts from the base weights (STaR, ReST-EM) to limit overfitting is from memory [R]; the live plan already does this. Havrilla et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04642) [S] find expert iteration performs best or on par with PPO, needs ~10^6 samples to converge, and models "fail to explore significantly beyond solutions already produced by SFT models"; the snippet that diversity rises over two rounds and then falls while maj@1 overfits is [R]. Expected gain here: unknown, since references are noisy (plan: seed programs with helper variables, one transport seed with a single demand row) and verification is on the value only. Cost: sampling-bound (plan: ~9 + 6.5 min of a ~46 min run). Compliance: train-side only; the live description names "searching training cases for the formulations that reproduce their given optimal values" as the intended use. Convergence: independently confirms the live plan's EI design and the playbook's "metric-aware training" lever; relates to curated G4 (pseudo-labelling needs agreement between independent signals, here the solver is the independent signal).

**F2. False-positive targets ("spurious programs") and how the literature controls them. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** The central risk of learning from outcomes is a wrong program that reaches the right value. Pasupat and Liang (https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06900) [S] show spurious logical forms dominate answer-only supervision and that executing candidates on fictitious worlds removed 92.1% of spurious forms while raising coverage from 53.5% to 76%. Guu et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.07926) [S] combine randomized exploration with a "meritocratic" update that spreads probability evenly over consistent programs. Min et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.04849) [S] use hard EM (gradient on the single most likely correct solution under the current model), +2 to 10 points across six tasks; this equals the live plan's "one target per row, tie-break by mean token logprob". Wang et al. 2023 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01161) [S] represent each program by its execution results under various inputs and run a majority vote to drop programs whose semantics differ from the rest; it is explicitly a training-time filter orthogonal to the search; this is the plan's perturbation-vector clustering. "Enforcing Consistency in Weakly Supervised Semantic Parsing" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05833) [S] biases the search so related inputs map the same phrase to the same sub-program (+10 points absolute on NLVR). Feng et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07515) [S]: even imperfect verifiers prevent collapse when filtering self-generated data. Risk specific to this challenge (ESTIMATE, from the plan's K=4): cluster voting over only 1 to 3 accepted programs per row is a weak signal; either draw more perturbation vectors (cheap, solver-only), accept only rows whose accepted candidates agree, or add the twin-consistency check of F6. Measure "acceptance precision" on the 248 seeds (share of V-accepted candidates with C=1 against the seed), exactly as the plan proposes. Compliance: train-side only; no test text involved. Convergence: confirms plan sections "false-positive EI targets" and "Metric-aware training".

**F3. Selecting among the model's own samples. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Self-consistency (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171) [S]: sample diverse paths, vote on the answer; +17.9 GSM8K, +11.0 SVAMP, +12.2 AQuA, +6.4 StrategyQA, +3.9 ARC-c. Execution MBR (MBR-Exec, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11454) [S] groups programs by execution results on a few inputs and marginalizes over equal semantics. CodeT (https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397) [S]: dual execution agreement (consistency with generated tests plus agreement with other samples), HumanEval pass@1 65.8 (+18.8 over code-davinci-002). LEVER (https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08468) [S]: train a verifier on (specification, program, execution result), combine its score with the LM likelihood and aggregate programs with identical execution results, +4.6 to 10.9 on Spider, WikiTQ, GSM8K, MBPP. AlphaCode (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814): ~99% of samples removed by example tests, the rest clustered by behaviour on generated inputs, one program taken from each of the 10 largest clusters (50 test inputs, 8,192 samples used for clustering; 34.2% solve rate at up to 1M samples) [R, from a lecture-slide snippet]. Large Language Monkeys (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787) [S]: coverage grows log-linearly with samples (SWE-bench-Lite 15.9% to 56% from 1 to 250 samples) but "majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples", so measure coverage (pass@K) and selection rate separately. SIRL (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11792v3) [S] votes on four signals extracted from the compiled model (objective value, direction, number of binary variables, number of integer variables) and reports the multi-signal vote clearly beats value-only voting on harder benchmarks (the "19 to 128%" range comes from the fetch summary of its Table 2; treat as [R] until read in the table). Application: the live plan's MBR utility (value agreement plus pairwise structure score) is already this family; the SIRL-style signature (direction, #int, #bin, #rows) is a cheap extra vote that needs no perturbation of eval numbers. Compliance: the live rules say "choosing among several outputs of the trained model for the same case is allowed"; the plan keeps solving candidates on perturbed test numbers off as grey. Convergence: confirms curated A3 (expected-utility/MBR decode) and the three-stage diagnosis (coverage / ranking / decode).

**F4. Candidate pool generation: sampler, temperature, pools from several models. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Epsilon sampling (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09860) [S] beat ancestral, nucleus and top-k sampling, and beam search, as the candidate generator for MBR on four language pairs (machine translation, not formulation; transfer is plausible, unproven). HF `generate` exposes `epsilon_cutoff` (suggested values 3e-4 to 9e-4) [R]. RFT [S] shows pooling rejection samples from different models increases the number of distinct paths and the gain, which supports a pool that unions the seeds-only SFT model and the EI model (the "union oracle" idea of curated D6). Large Language Monkeys [S]: coverage follows an exponentiated power law in the number of samples, so coverage on large (>=6 variable) problems is the quantity to track by size bucket. Typical temperatures in this literature are 0.7 to 1.0 with top-p 0.95 (plan: 0.8 train, 0.7 test) [R]. Compliance: none (training or per-row decode).

**F5. Concrete hypotheses for the live plan (to test, in this order). [UNTESTED HYPOTHESIS] 2026-10-02.** Each rests on F1 to F4 and is train-side or per-row. (a) Label-shuffle negative control: run one EI round with each row's label replaced by another row's; acceptance yield must collapse; if it does not, the verifier leaks (cheap, ~5 min on the dev slice; motivated by the Qwen2.5 spurious-reward and contamination results of F15). (b) Success-adaptive K on the unresolved train rows: after round 1 only ~1,200 rows lack a target, so K can rise from 4 to 8 to 16 for them (about 10k to 19k sequences versus 4.8k planned); adaptivity conditioned on training success is data-determined, not clock-determined, so it does not violate CLAUDE.md section 3 rule 5. (c) Learned selector (LEVER-style): train a small model (logistic regression or GBDT, seeded) on round-1 samples of the non-seed rows only (the round-1 model was fitted on seeds, so those rows are unbiased), label = V-correct against the known optimal value (all rows have it), features = agreement-cluster fraction, #accepted peers, mean token logprob, length, #int, #bin, #rows, finite-optimum under real numbers; apply to each test row's own candidates. Seed rows must be excluded from the selector's training set (they were in the SFT set). Round-2/3 samples are biased (those rows were in training), so do not use them. Compliance: a trained model choosing among the model's own outputs for the same case. (d) Union candidate pool from the seeds-only and the EI model at test time (measure union pass@K). (e) Keep at most one target per row is the plan default; RFT and "solution diversification" (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26548) [S: solution buffer plus discriminator, gains in weakly supervised math word problems] suggest an A/B with up to 2 *structurally distinct* accepted targets per row, at the risk of diluting imitation of the reference style that the structure term rewards.

**F6. Train-side label expansion: twin transfer, "learning by fixing", twin-consistency. [UNTESTED HYPOTHESIS] 2026-10-02.** Setting: in `grounded_ip_formulation` references are per-problem random but `numbers_json` dedupes by value, so a value maps to exactly one ref per problem; the plan's audit found 313 scenario groups with both table and prose versions and 1,434 non-seed rows sharing a group with a seed. Hypothesis 1 (twin transfer): when a non-seed train problem has the same multiset of number values as a seed problem, rewrite the seed formulation by mapping refs through values, solve it, and accept it as that row's target if the optimum equals the row's label (verification by the given weak label). This is "label merging across repeated statements" applied to programs; it can only add verified rows and needs no model. A ~10-line local check on train.csv (count non-seed rows whose number-value multiset equals a seed's) sizes the gain; I could not run code in this session. Hypothesis 2 (learning by fixing, https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10582 [S]): for rows where no sample reaches the value, apply a small deterministic edit search to the model's best near-miss (flip a relation sense, toggle int/bin, drop or add one candidate row from the sample pool) and accept edits that reproduce the label, then filter with F2; in the cited paper only final answers supervise and the memory buffer of fixes widens diversity. Hypothesis 3 (consistency across related inputs): use agreement of structure signatures between twin problems as an acceptance filter (https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05833 [S]). Compliance: all three touch training cases only and use only given labels; the live text explicitly allows searching training cases for formulations that reproduce their optimal values and forbids the same for evaluation cases, so never run any of this on test rows, and state in the docstring that these steps build training targets (they are not output repair). Risk: twins may differ in a constraint that does not change the optimum, creating false positives (F2 filters); precision can be measured on the seeds.

**F7. RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO) on small budgets: why expert iteration first. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** GRPO biases: Dr. GRPO (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783) [S] identifies a length bias and a std-normalization bias and removes them; TRL's GRPOTrainer exposes `loss_type` in {grpo, dapo, dr_grpo, sapo}, `num_generations` (default 8), `scale_rewards`, and treats vLLM as optional-but-recommended because "generation is the bottleneck in online RL" (https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/grpo_trainer) [S]. Small-scale evidence: a 1.5B model with GRPO used 4 A40 GPUs for 24 h ($42) on 7,000 samples, improved fast (AMC23 63 to 80) and then showed "optimization instability and length constraints" with longer training (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16219) [S]; Tina (https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15777) [S] reports LoRA-based RL on a 1.5B model for ~$9 and hypothesizes LoRA adapts the format RL rewards while preserving knowledge; Thinking Machines (https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/) [S] show rank-1 LoRA matches full fine-tuning in RL because policy gradients carry ~1 bit per episode. Yue et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) [S]: RLVR raises pass@1 but base models reach equal or higher pass@k at large k, and "distillation can introduce new reasoning patterns" while RLVR mostly sharpens. Havrilla [S] (F1): EI is as sample-efficient as PPO. Mismatch note: "Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16" (https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26788v1) [S] argues bf16 rollouts versus the training engine diverge and fp16's 10 mantissa bits fix it, which matters for GRPO importance ratios but not for EI (plain SFT on accepted samples). Verdict for a 1 h A10G budget with HF `generate` rollouts and no vLLM: EI plus selection is the right use of time; RL cannot create coverage that sampling lacks (the live problem is coverage on large problems). If GRPO is tried anyway: hand-rolled (TRL is not on the allowed list, F27), LoRA r in 1 to 8, fixed step count, reward = V-exact AND finiteness under perturbation (not value closeness), score old log-probs with the training forward. Compliance: in-script RL is training; fix all seeds and steps.

**F8. Autocast semantics and the "low-precision copy for generation" rule. [GENERAL ML PRINCIPLE] 2026-10-02.** From PyTorch's autocast implementation (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytorch/pytorch/main/aten/src/ATen/autocast_mode.cpp) [S]: a lower-precision cast of an fp32 weight is cached only when the tensor `requires_grad()`, is a leaf, is not a view, `cache_enabled` is set, and inference mode is NOT active; the cache lives until the outermost autocast context exits. Consequences: (i) under `torch.inference_mode()` or with frozen parameters (`requires_grad=False`) an fp32 model under autocast re-casts every weight at every op of every decode step; (ii) with grad-enabled parameters under `no_grad`, one outer autocast around the whole `generate` call keeps the cache, but a per-step autocast does not. Decode is memory-bound, so uncached weight traffic is about 4 bytes read + 2 written + 2 read = 8 bytes/parameter/step versus 2 for a bf16 copy (ESTIMATE): ~4 GB per step for 0.5B (about 6.6 ms at 600 GB/s) and ~12 GB per step for 1.5B (about 20 ms) before launch overhead. Rule: for sampling, run a bf16 (A10G) or fp16 (T4) copy of the weights in `inference_mode`, no autocast; keep the fp32 master only for training. If training must continue afterwards, take `copy.deepcopy(model).to(dtype)` (a bf16-to-fp32 cast does not restore precision); the live plan restarts from base each round, so converting in place is safe there. Convergence: the live `solution.py` already converts to bf16 for generation (line ~596), independently confirming this practice; PyTorch's own amp notes advise against calling `.half()` on a model that is also trained under autocast, which is why the copy must be separate from the training module [R].

**F9. HF generate pitfalls that cost time or break structured outputs. [GENERAL ML PRINCIPLE] 2026-10-02.** (1) Wasted decode steps. A static batch runs until its longest member finishes. With the plan's target-length statistics (median 112, p90 196, p99 366, max 463 tokens; batches of 256) the expected maximum in a batch is near p99.6 to max, about 366 to 463 steps versus a mean need of ~125, so ~3 to 3.7x of the decode compute is spent on finished rows (ESTIMATE from plan data; padding rows are not freed). Fixes in order of effort: set `max_new_tokens` from the train-target length p99.9 plus margin (not 768), use smaller batches for long-expected inputs, compact finished sequences out of the batch (index-select the KV cache every N steps; ~40 lines, deterministic), or use continuous batching (F10). (2) Inherited generation config. `Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct` ships `repetition_penalty 1.1, top_k 20, top_p 0.8, temperature 0.7, eos_token_id [151645, 151643]`, while `Qwen2.5-0.5B` (base) ships `do_sample false, max_new_tokens 2048, eos_token_id 151643` (https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct/raw/main/generation_config.json, https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B/raw/main/generation_config.json) [S]. A repetition penalty silently breaks outputs that legitimately repeat digits and variable names; always pass `repetition_penalty=1.0, top_k=0` explicitly and assert the effective config before sampling. The EOS used in training targets must equal what `generate` stops on (TRL's SFT docs warn about exactly this for Qwen, https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/sft_trainer [S]); assert EOS was reached and log truncations. (3) Prefill logits. Transformers >= 4.45 has `logits_to_keep` (formerly `num_logits_to_keep`) so prefill need not materialize [batch, prompt_len, vocab] logits; with vocab 151,936, batch 256 and 400 prompt tokens that tensor would be ~62 GB in fp32 (ESTIMATE), so verify peak memory empirically in any custom loop (HF issue 35629 asks whether `generate` really applies it) [R]. (4) `padding_side="left"` for decoder-only batches and explicit `pad_token_id` [R]. (5) `num_return_sequences=K` repeats the prefill K times (minor next to decode). (6) Allocator fragmentation with the growing dynamic KV cache: `PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True` set before CUDA init is the usual remedy [R; the PyTorch page did not load for verification]. (7) Speculative and prompt-lookup decoding do not support batched inputs (HF optimization guide, https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/en/llm_optims [S]), so they do not help batched sampling.

**F10. Static KV cache, torch.compile and continuous batching in transformers. [UNTESTED HYPOTHESIS] 2026-10-02.** The HF optimization guide [S] says a static KV cache plus `torch.compile(mode="reduce-overhead", fullgraph=True)` can give up to ~4x speedup (smaller for larger models), recompiles whenever batch size or maximum output length changes, and recommends left-padding to a limited set of lengths (`pad_to_multiple_of`). Current docs also describe `model.generate_batch(...)` with continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching (shared prompt KV across requests), optional CUDA graphs and compile levels, per-request sampling parameters, and `return_logprobs` (https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/en/continuous_batching) [S]. It works with `sdpa` but "much better" with flash attention (not available on T4); `auto_switch_to_flash` swaps the backend when flash is importable. Open questions (all need a local profile on the pinned transformers version of the Kaggle image, which I cannot see): sampling support with K samples per prompt, version availability, bitwise reproducibility, and whether an automatic backend switch counts as "backend logic" for the Deterministic Execution check (CLAUDE.md section 3 flags hardware/backend branching; keep one fixed configuration, no try/except fallback). Expected gain: removes the wasted-step factor of F9 and shares prompt KV across the K samples; unknown until measured. Compliance: no change to the trained model; keep it out of the plan until a profile proves it.

**F11. Batch composition changes numerics and RNG consumption; implications for the half-rows test. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Thinking Machines (https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/) [S] show the dominant source of LLM-inference nondeterminism is lack of batch invariance (RMSNorm, matmul and attention reductions change with batch size), and that even temperature-0 decoding produced 80 distinct completions out of 1,000 under varying load until batch-invariant kernels were used. My reading (UNTESTED): the plan's per-batch seeding (`torch.manual_seed(SEED + batch_idx)` before each `generate`) and any batching by length tie each row's samples to which other rows share its batch, so dropping half the rows will change some strings even with fixed seeds, without any cross-row aggregation. The platform check targets runtime-dependent plans, and the repo's half-rows test targets whole-test aggregation; document this in the docstring, compare the half-rows result on optimum agreement rather than string identity, and keep batch construction a deterministic function of the data order only. Run-to-run identity with the same inputs should hold under the fixed plan.

**F12. Memory-safe language-model loss for large vocabularies (the T4 OOM). [GENERAL ML PRINCIPLE] 2026-10-02.** With vocabulary 151,936 the fp32 logits tensor for batch 8 x 1,024 tokens is 4.97 GB and gradients double it (https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02609v1) [S]; for the plan's micro-batch 8 x 1,280 it is ~6.2 GB per copy (ESTIMATE), which is consistent with the `OutOfMemory` in backward at the first SFT stage in `challenges/grounded_ip_formulation/kaggle_out/gip-dev1.log` (T4, fp16 autocast) [S, local log]. TRL's SFTTrainer now defaults to `loss_type="chunked_nll"`: the `lm_head` projection is computed only on non-ignored (target) tokens and cross-entropy is processed in token chunks so peak memory no longer scales with vocab x sequence (https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/sft_trainer) [S]; Liger's fused linear cross-entropy reports >4x memory reduction at 128k vocab [R, snippet]. Original-implementation recipe (plain torch, no extra libraries): run the transformer body, gather hidden states at positions where the label is not -100, push chunks of a few thousand positions through `lm_head` and CE inside `torch.utils.checkpoint` so chunk logits are recomputed in backward, and divide the summed loss by the total target tokens of the whole accumulated batch (averaging per-micro-batch means mis-weights uneven token counts; the Transformers gradient-accumulation fix of 2024 addressed this [R]). Side benefit (ESTIMATE): for Qwen2.5-0.5B the tied lm_head is 136M of 494M parameters (~27% of forward FLOPs per token) and target tokens are ~23% of each sequence (target median 112 vs prompt ~370 tokens per the plan), so target-only projection cuts total training FLOPs by roughly 19%. Compliance: none. Note interaction with F17 (loss over prompt tokens would forfeit part of this saving).

**F13. Precision rules: pure-bf16 weight updates silently cancel; fp16 on T4. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** "Revisiting BFloat16 Training" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06192) [S]: with round-to-nearest, 16-bit weight updates are often cancelled; stochastic rounding or Kahan summation recovers accuracy (up to +7 points in 16-bit training). torchao documents optional bf16 stochastic rounding for this reason and low-bit AdamW (8-bit/4-bit; one ViT-H benchmark 12.23 GB to 8.33/7.72 GB of memory) but requires PyTorch >= 2.3 and a working `torch.compile` (https://github.com/pytorch/ao/tree/main/torchao/optim) [S]. Arithmetic for the plan's capacity rung "Qwen2.5-1.5B, bf16 params + fp32 Adam states" (ESTIMATE): bf16 has 7 explicit mantissa bits; for a weight near 0.03 the spacing is 2^-13 = 1.2e-4, so an Adam step of ~5e-5 (LR 5e-5, normalized step <= 1) is below half-spacing (6.1e-5) and is rounded away; every weight with |w| above ~0.016 would not move, and the cosine schedule makes it worse. Typical LLM weight scales (~0.02 to 0.04) mean most weights would freeze, with training loss still decreasing a little via small-weight tensors, which is exactly the failure that looks "fine" until compared. Remedies: fp32 master weights (fine for 0.5B: ~8 GB), stochastic-rounding or Kahan updates (torchao optimizers, if the library is acceptable, see F27), or LoRA with fp32 adapters over a frozen bf16 base (F16). T4 (Turing): no bf16 tensor cores and no FlashAttention-2 (Ampere or newer); use fp16 + GradScaler and SDPA (search-snippet sources, https://www.baseten.co/blog/comparing-nvidia-gpus-for-ai-t4-vs-a10/ for bandwidth and FP16 numbers) [R]; the AIMO-1 winners quantized to 8-bit partly because "T4 GPUs lack bfloat16 support" (https://huggingface.co/blog/winning-aimo-progress-prize) [S]. Peak spec numbers are close (A10G ~70 FP16 tensor TFLOPS vs T4 65) but A10G has ~600 GB/s versus ~300 to 320 GB/s, so decode is ~1.9x slower on T4 and compute-bound training several times slower in practice (ESTIMATE; T4 is power-limited) [R]. Qwen-specific fp16 overflow evidence is anecdotal (NaN/"!!!!" outputs reported for Qwen2-VL and some Qwen2.5 sizes) [R]; the plan's guard (assert finite loss/logits, switch dev and ship together to bf16) is correct. Compliance: any dev-vs-ship precision constant must be a top-of-file constant restored before submission (CLAUDE.md section 3 rule 3).

Qwen2.5-0.5B: 24 layers, 2 KV heads, head_dim 64 gives 12,288 B (12 KB). Qwen2.5-1.5B: 28 layers, 2 KV heads, head_dim 128 gives 28,672 B (28 KB). SmolLM2-1.7B: 24 layers, 32 KV heads (full multi-head), head_dim 64 gives 196,608 B (192 KB). For 256 sequences x 1,300 tokens (332,800 tokens): Qwen2.5-0.5B ~4.1 GB, Qwen2.5-1.5B ~9.5 GB, SmolLM2-360M ~13.6 GB, Qwen3-0.6B ~38 GB (does not fit 24 GB; ~64 sequences would), SmolLM2-1.7B ~65 GB (ESTIMATE). **F15. Base-model contamination and spurious-reward results; a negative control. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** "Reasoning or Memorization?" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10532) [S] argues Qwen2.5's RL gains on MATH-500, AMC and AIME are inflated by pretraining contamination: on a leakage-free arithmetic set only accurate rewards gave steady gains beyond the base model while random or incorrect rewards gave none. "Spurious Rewards" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947) [S]: random rewards improved Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500 (vs 29.1 with true rewards) through GRPO clipping bias and amplified code-reasoning behaviour, and the effect failed to transfer to Llama3 or OLMo2. Implication: gains from self-training on a Qwen model should always be checked against a control where the verifier signal is destroyed (F5a: shuffled labels, or a trivial constant reward) before being believed; this guards both the verifier code and the interpretation. For the live task the contamination worry (that the published problem collection could sit in web pretraining data) cannot be resolved by us; the description permits open-weight decoders and "general knowledge of optimization modeling", and the plan states no OR-tuned checkpoint is used. Compliance: no action beyond the docstring statement.

**F16. LoRA versus full fine-tuning: a decision rule. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Thinking Machines, "LoRA Without Regret" (https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/) [S]: LoRA matches full fine-tuning in sample efficiency and final quality when (a) it is applied to all layers, especially MLPs (attention-only LoRA underperforms even at matched parameter counts) and (b) it is not capacity-constrained (trainable parameters exceed the information in the data); the optimal LoRA learning rate is ~10x the full-FT rate (~15x for ~100-step runs) and roughly independent of rank thanks to 1/r scaling; LoRA pays a larger loss penalty at big batch sizes; rank-1 suffices for RL. Biderman et al., "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673) [S]: at ~100K instruction pairs and 20B continued-pretraining tokens LoRA substantially underperforms full fine-tuning in code and math (full FT learns weight perturbations of rank 10 to 100x higher) but forgets less. QLoRA (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314) [S]: NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers, and LoRA on all linear layers beats selected layers. TRL's guidance for adapters is a higher LR, about 1e-4 [S]. Reconciliation: the capacity condition is the switch. At 248 to ~3,000 sequences (~0.3 to 1.5M target tokens) all-linear LoRA with r = 32 to 64 (about 35M trainable parameters for a 0.5B Qwen, ESTIMATE) has far more capacity than the data carries, so parity with full FT is expected; at 100K+ examples full FT wins. Curated E6 says "full fine-tune beats LoRA on small data", which conflicts mildly: a likely cause is LoRA run at the full-FT learning rate (under-trained) or a random head without `modules_to_save`; worth one paired A/B with LR 10x and all-linear targets. Practical notes: `peft` is not on the project's allowed list (F27), so a hand-rolled LoRA (A random init, B zero init, scale alpha/r, no LoRA on the tied embedding/lm_head) is the compliant route; merge the adapters into the base weights before sampling (PEFT docs, https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/developer_guides/lora [S]: unmerged adapters add inference latency, `merge_and_unload()` removes it). LoRA is what makes a 1.5B to 3B rung fit 16 to 24 GB without the pure-bf16 problem of F13. Compliance: still genuine fine-tuning of public weights fitted on supplied data.

**F17. Loss over prompt tokens ("instruction modelling") as a small-data regularizer. [UNTESTED HYPOTHESIS] 2026-10-02.** Shi et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14394) [S]: computing loss on instruction tokens too improves results when instructions are long relative to outputs and when training data is small (reported as reducing overfitting; best-case gains were large on AlpacaEval). The live data sit in that regime (prompt ~370 tokens versus target ~112; 248 to ~3,000 sequences). A/B: loss weight on prompt tokens 0 (plan default), 0.1, 1.0; the extra tokens are the *train* problem texts only (no test text is used, so §2.3 #5 is not touched). Cost: forfeits the F12 saving (full-sequence lm_head) and adds ~20% to 40% training time (ESTIMATE). Expected gain: unknown; the value is as an anti-memorization regularizer when the same scenarios recur in prose and table form. TRL's `completion_only_loss` default is the opposite convention [S].

**F18. Grammar-constrained decoding: what the literature shows. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Geng et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13971) [S]: input-dependent grammars improve information extraction, entity disambiguation and constituency parsing and can beat task-specific fine-tuned baselines, mainly where little data or fine-tuning is available. PICARD (https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05093) [S]: incremental parsing rejects inadmissible tokens at each step and "transforms fine-tuned T5 models with passable performance into state-of-the-art" on Spider and CoSQL. Grammar-Aligned Decoding (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21047) [S]: standard GCD "distorts the LLM's distribution", producing grammatical outputs with likelihoods not proportional to the model's; ASAp corrects this. XGrammar (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15100) [S]: a CFG engine claiming up to 100x speedup and near-zero overhead in serving stacks (HF integration not confirmed from the abstract). Reading for fine-tuned small LMs: the benefit is largest when the model is weak or validity is the bottleneck; after SFT on >1k programs, an invalid-program rate in the low teens (plan expectation: validity >= 85%) is mostly removed by the K-sample validity filter. Expected gain here: small; cost: custom logits processor (hand-rolled, since outlines/xgrammar are not on the allowed list).

**F19. Why constrained decoding may read as rule-based repair here, and the compliant substitutes. [UNTESTED HYPOTHESIS] 2026-10-02.** This is my reading, not a platform ruling. The live rules ban "producing or repairing formulations with hand-written parsing rules or templates (keyword-to-constraint tables, regular-expression fixes of the model's output, positional defaults)" and say such rules "may build model inputs or features only". A decode-time grammar mask is a hand-written rule set that forbids or forces tokens, so a reviewer can read it as rule-assisted production even though nothing is edited after the fact; masking refs to the existing x<k> set is the most exposed variant because it encodes task knowledge. The plan already classifies it as grey and defers (Open question 4). Substitutes that stay inside the stated rules: (a) train-time validity: reject invalid sampled programs as EI targets and mask nothing at decode; (b) test-time choice among K samples by validity and value agreement ("choosing among outputs ... is allowed"); (c) an auxiliary or contrastive loss lowering likelihood of invalid programs; (d) fix the output grammar only by training examples. Needs written reviewer approval (saved under the challenge reports, CLAUDE.md 2.3A) before any decode-time constraint. Do not use any ORLM/LLMOPT-style OR-specialised model's constrained templates.

**F20. Fine-tuning stability of pretrained encoders. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** Mosbach et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04884) [S]: failed fine-tuning runs come from optimization difficulties (vanishing gradients) and generalization variance, not catastrophic forgetting or small data; remedies are Adam bias correction, longer training and warmup. Zhang et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05987) [S]: biased gradient estimation in the old BERTAdam, limited usefulness of the top layers (re-initializing them helps) and too-short schedules; fixing these makes many few-sample tricks unnecessary. PyTorch AdamW already applies bias correction, so the practical items left are warmup (about 6 to 10% of steps), enough epochs/steps, and optional top-layer re-initialization on tiny sets. Multi-sample dropout (https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09788) [S]: several dropout masks after the encoder with shared head weights and averaged losses, faster convergence at negligible cost because the encoder runs once. AWP (https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05884) [S] was proposed for adversarial robustness in vision; extra forward/backward overhead is not stated in the abstract, and Kaggle reports are mixed (F21). Model soups (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05482) [S]: averaging weights of models fine-tuned from the same pretrained checkpoint (uniform or greedy soup) gives gains without extra inference cost. Convergence: confirms curated E-C (warmup + LLRD + EMA + multi-sample dropout) and E-A/D4 (greedy soup). Compliance: none.

**F21. Kaggle text-competition recipes (non-Eris, method only). [COMPETITION PATTERN] 2026-10-02.** (a) CommonLit Readability top solutions: roberta-large or deberta-large with mean pooling, layer-wise LR decay (start ~3e-5, multiplier ~0.975), frequent mid-epoch validation (every 10 to 600 steps depending on size), pseudo-labelling used by some [R, via search snippets]. (b) Feedback Prize "Predicting Effective Arguments" 1st place (Team Hydrogen): deberta-(v3)-large backbones, all discourse units of an essay trained together as one sequence, different LRs for backbone and head, dropout changes, masking augmentation, a post-processing shift toward the train mean; in another top solution FGM and AWP did not help while SiFT did; pseudo-labelling had mixed success [R, search snippets; the discussion pages did not load]. (c) PII Data Detection 1st place: five DeBERTa-v3-large variants with multi-sample dropout, a BiLSTM head, knowledge distillation from the best model, and name-swap augmentation, trained on 8x A100 40 GB; it also used extra datasets, which Eris bans (https://github.com/bogoconic1/pii-detection-1st-place) [S]. (d) LMSYS Chatbot Arena 1st place: 70B-class models trained in 5-fold, distilled with a KL loss into Gemma2-9B per fold, LoRA layers of the five students averaged into one model, on 8x A100 80 GB; other teams used pseudo-labelling and 33B models on 2x T4 (https://nebius.com/blog/posts/chatbot-arena-competition-review) [S]. **F22. Encoder versus decoder-LLM classifiers; large versus base. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** DeBERTaV3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09543) [S] replaces masked-LM pretraining with replaced-token detection plus gradient-disentangled embedding sharing; DeBERTaV3-large averages 91.37 on GLUE (+1.37 over DeBERTa), and mDeBERTa-base reaches 79.8% zero-shot cross-lingual XNLI (+3.6 over XLM-R base). ModernBERT (https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663) [S abstract] offers 8,192-token context, 2T training tokens and efficiency; its GLUE parity with DeBERTaV3 is not verified here [R]. For small labelled sets, attaching a classification head to a decoder LLM beat instruction-style prompting and matched or exceeded domain encoders: with 1,481 and 1,731 training examples, LoRA r=16 (dropout 5%), 4-bit weights, 8-bit optimizers on a 24 GB GPU, Llama-3.2-3B reached F1 0.86 versus PatentBERT 0.854 while training ~12M versus 346M parameters (https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12677v1) [S]. Rule of thumb (GENERAL, partly [R]): prefer deberta-v3-large when the text fits 512 tokens and compute allows 5 folds x seeds in an hour; use base size or LLRD 0.9 to 0.95, LR 1e-5 to 2e-5 and many seeds below ~500 labelled examples where large models are unstable (F20); use ModernBERT or windowed DeBERTa for long documents; reserve decoder-LLM LoRA classifiers for tasks that need world knowledge and tolerate 1 to 2 folds. Seed/fold ensembling should average logits (curated E-A); soups are a free variant (F20). Compliance: genuine fine-tuning.

**F23. Hard-negative mining and cross-encoder training for retrieval/ranking. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** RocketQA (https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191) [S]: cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives (a cross-encoder filters likely false negatives), and cross-encoder pseudo-labels on unlabelled passages. NV-Retriever (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15831) [S]: naive top-k hard negatives include false negatives; "positive-aware" mining (TopK-MarginPos, TopK-PercPos) anchors the cutoff to the positive's score; MTEB-Retrieval 60.9 at publication. Sentence-Transformers' `mine_hard_negatives` exposes the same knobs: `range_min`, `range_max`, `max_score`, `min_score`, `absolute_margin`, `relative_margin` (e.g. 0.05 drops negatives scoring above positive - |positive| x 0.05), `sampling_strategy` top or random, `num_negatives` (default 3), and triplet/n-tuple/labeled formats (https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/util/hard_negatives.html) [S]; the library is not on the project's allowed list (F27), so the mining is a few lines of matrix multiply and top-k. "Building Better Encoder-only Cross-Encoders" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03010) [S]: across 162 configurations, relative-comparison objectives (pairwise MarginMSE, listwise InfoNCE) consistently beat pointwise objectives, switching the loss gave a gain comparable to a larger backbone, and with a matched negative pool a simple pairwise hinge with ColBERTv2 hard negatives matched listwise LLM distillation. "Scaling Laws for Cross-Encoder Reranking" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04816) [S]: ranking quality follows power laws up to ~150M parameters, with retrieval metrics often favouring data scaling over model scaling. Eris-specific cautions (GENERAL, my reading): (i) mine negatives from an out-of-fold retriever (a retriever fine-tuned on those very queries over-scores train positives and defeats margin filters, same logic as curated V8); (ii) exclude negatives that share a group or scenario with the query (union-find groups as in V2) since they are the likeliest false negatives; (iii) keep the query's own document out of its bank; (iv) a frozen off-the-shelf retriever plus a *trained* cross-encoder or fine-tuned bi-encoder is the accepted architecture (CLAUDE.md 6.4); BM25 and TF-IDF stay features. Convergence: confirms curated G14 (refresh hard negatives from the model's own scores), the three-stage diagnosis and the existing retrieval family guide; adds the false-negative controls and the loss ordering.

**F24. Kaggle retrieval-and-rerank patterns. [COMPETITION PATTERN] 2026-10-02.** Learning Equality Curriculum Recommendations (public GitHub write-ups, https://github.com/nlztrk/Learning-Equality-Curriculum-Recommendations did not load; details via search snippets) [R]: stage 1 a sentence-transformer bi-encoder, kNN (RAPIDS) to the nearest 100 content items, stage 2 a cross-encoder initialized from the stage-1 model (sequence length 128, 15 epochs, batch 256, 5% warmup), a decision threshold tuned on the hold-out to maximize F2, empty predictions imputed with the top-scoring items, grouped stratified folds by topic and language. **F25. Small-data NLP: what to spend effort on. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** (a) Augmentation of real samples: Longpre et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01764) [S] found EDA and back-translation "fail to consistently improve performance for pretrained transformers" (BERT, XLNet, RoBERTa; 5 tasks, 6 datasets) even with limited data. Prefer augmentations tied to the task's invariances (entity or name swaps as in the PII 1st place [S], reference-index permutation in the live plan, which is an exact relabelling) over generic text noise; generating new text with a model is synthetic data and banned. (b) Split protocol: Gorman and Bedrick (https://aclanthology.org/P19-1267/) [S] show system rankings change across random splits on the same data, so repeat the split (2 to 3 seeds) and report spread (curated V4). With repeated sentences, split by group (union-find on first sentence, number set or near-duplicates). (c) Repeated or conflicting labels for the same text: merge them into a soft label (mean) or a multi-head target rather than a majority vote; Davani et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05719) [S] show predicting each annotator's label as a separate sub-task over a shared representation beats aggregating first and gives uncertainty correlated with disagreement. (d) Label errors: Confident Learning (https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00068) [S] uses out-of-sample predicted probabilities and per-class thresholds to flag mislabeled rows, then prunes; run it on out-of-fold predictions of train only. (e) Continued masked-LM pretraining on train inputs (E5) and LP-FT (E-B) are already curated; the decision rule is unchanged. Compliance: all train-only.

**F26. Text-to-optimization-model literature: background only. [PUBLIC RESEARCH] 2026-10-02.** What makes the task hard: many programs share an optimum (value-only supervision is ambiguous), reference quality is poor (a survey of eight benchmarks, with 11 human experts and >=3 cross-validations per error, reports error rates of at least IndustryOR 54.0%, NL4Opt 26.4%, ComplexLP 23.7%, ComplexOR 24.3%, NLP4LP 21.7%, ReSocratic 16.0%, EasyLP 8.13%; https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10047 [S]), long-tail problem types, numeric grounding, and size generalization from small to large models. **F28. AIMO-1 (NuminaMath) pattern. [COMPETITION PATTERN] 2026-10-02.** The winning pipeline (https://huggingface.co/blog/winning-aimo-progress-prize) [S] used two-stage SFT (several hundred thousand chain-of-thought problems, then ~60k tool-integrated problems; LR 2e-5 in both stages; block sizes 2048 and 1024; 3 and 4 epochs) and an inference scheme called SC-TIR: N = 48 sampled candidates, depth M = 4 with execution feedback (tracebacks appended), then majority voting; the model was quantized to 8-bit because T4 GPUs lack bf16 and fp32 exceeded VRAM; validation used small curated sets (AMC 83 problems, AIME 90) with explicit overfitting caution given two submissions per day.
